A bounded coaching skill

Find the problem worth solving.
Grow while you do it.

Most good work is bigger than the window people see it through. This skill helps you and your agent carve one clear offer out of it, for one person, under a name they would recognize.

It reads where you start, coaches five passes with your attempt first, and stops on a fixed rule. You get two cards back: one for the work, one for you.

Report them separately

The work is one climb.
You are another.

A sharp offer you can't explain won't survive the first hard question. A clear head with no offer ships nothing. The skill tracks both, and never lets one stand in for the other.

Placements describe one person on one piece of work on one day. They are not grades of ability.

W

The work

One specific customer, the expensive problem they have today, a name they would recognize, and evidence that it lands. It ends as a fit card, even when the answer is "no fit yet."

P

The person

Whether you can explain the fit, defend it, and choose on purpose. It ends as a growth card: where you started, where you got to, every dip, and every choice with your reason.

Five passes, one exit

Carve. Choose.
Stop on purpose.

Each pass removes stone. Each pairs a piece of work with a teaching move and a gate. You attempt first, the agent responds, and you make the call.

The exit rule comes first.

Aim for the launch zone. Three rounds at most.

A failed gate sends you back to that pass only, never through all five again. If a full round doesn't move the work, the problem is upstream: the wrong customer or the wrong pain. The skill says so and stops.

The default target

The launch zone.

Clear enough to put in front of strangers in a campaign. The run stops there, because coaching past it is just polishing.

  • Customer. One role, one setting, one painful moment.
  • Problem. The stuck job in their words, with a cost.
  • Name. A stranger can guess the problem from it.
  • Picture. Before and after, read in ten seconds.
  • Defense. You can explain every line, and the riskiest bet.
  • Signal. One real signal, or a plan to get one in week one.

  1. Find the expensive problem.

    A specific person, the job they're stuck on, and what it costs them today.

    Teaching moveYou write the pain sentence first, then compare it with a feature-shaped one.

    GateRole, job and cost. Unknown cost means ask, never invent.

  2. Name it for the customer.

    Three candidate names and a one-line promise, each tested against the pain.

    Teaching moveYou predict what a stranger would guess before hearing the agent's view.

    GateA stranger could guess the problem from the name alone.

  3. Show it in one picture.

    The customer's day, before and after. No architecture.

    Teaching moveYou sketch first. Boxes and arrows mean leftover stone.

    GateUnderstood in about ten seconds.

  4. Teach the why.

    Why this fit should hold, and the one assumption that would break it.

    Teaching moveYou explain it back. The agent fixes only the gap and plays skeptic once.

    GateYou name the riskiest assumption without being told.

  5. Test against the target.

    Score the fit card against the agreed target, with a real signal where one exists.

    Teaching moveYou rate your confidence first. The gap between guess and result is the lesson.

    GateIn the launch zone, or round budget reached.

Every pass ends with your choice. The log keeps what you picked, what you turned down, and your reason in your words. No reason, no entry: the agent asks one more question instead.

Every run ends with both cards, including runs that miss. A stopped run with an honest growth card is a better result than a polished guess.

Read through conversation, not a quiz

What it reads
in you.

Before the first pass, a few plain questions. After every pass, a fresh placement on five independent dimensions, each with its evidence. Nothing is scored out loud during the run.

DimensionEarlyDevelopingStrong

  1. Customer

    Early"Developers", "universities", "everyone"

    DevelopingA role in a setting

    StrongA specific person at the moment the pain hits

  2. Problem

    EarlyLists features

    DevelopingNames a pain

    StrongNames the pain and what it costs today

  3. Naming

    EarlyPicks what sounds good

    DevelopingSpots mechanism words

    StrongTests names against the pain sentence

  4. Reasoning

    EarlyStates the answer

    DevelopingGives a reason

    StrongNames the riskiest assumption unprompted

  5. Evidence

    Early"People liked it"

    DevelopingSets an observable target

    StrongReads a miss and picks the right pass to revisit

Never averaged into one number. A run can end below where it started on a dimension, and the card says so.

Attempt first, always

How it
teaches.

Answer-first help builds dependence. Here you try, and the agent responds to what you actually did. The support fades as you take over.

Round 1Agent showsA short worked example, then your turn.
Round 2TogetherYou lead. Prompts only at the gates.
Round 3You leadThe agent checks gates and asks why.

The bar shows how much of the work the agent carries. A returning user starts at the level their last growth card recommends.

Eight teaching moves

  1. Attempt first Ask for the user's answer before offering yoursEvery pass
  2. Contrast pairs Show a weak and a strong version side by side; the user says which and whyFind, Name
  3. One step up Pitch each question just above where the user is nowEvery pass
  4. Explain it back The user restates the idea; correct only what is missingTeach
  5. Safe failure Let a weak choice reach Test when the stakes are low, then debriefTest
  6. Confidence check The user rates certainty before the result, then comparesTest
  7. Name the dip Point out a drop and its likely causeEvery checkpoint
  8. Spaced return Open the next run on the weakest dimension from last timeNext run

Point first. One question. A little dry.

It sounds like
a good coach.

People stay with a coach who is quick and has a pulse. The first sentence carries the point. Humor goes at the situation, the product, or the agent itself. Never at you.

Opening questions

  1. Who uses this first? Describe their worst Tuesday.
  2. What goes wrong for them if this doesn't exist?
  3. Give me one sentence for a slide. Go.
  4. Why hasn't anyone picked it up yet?
  5. What would make you say this worked, three months from now?

Why it
isn't landing.

When a gate fails, the agent names the symptom, the likely cause, and the one pass to revisit. This is where most of the learning happens.

The bar it
has to clear.

Twelve fictional portfolios with planted traps, four simulated learners with scripted regressions, five real people, and a no-skill control. Two judges score blind. The least obvious test is the most important: a week later, can you do it without the skill?

TestPasses ifGuards against
Stops on time100% of runs end by round 3, and no run repeats all five passes after a failed gateEndless loops
Stops in the zoneRuns end within one pass of meeting all six launch-zone items, and no run that misses an item claims the zonePolishing forever, or declaring victory early
Stranger test7 of 10 strangers name the problem from the name and promise alone, beating the original namePretty names that say nothing
Ten-second read8 of 10 viewers describe the before and after picture correctly after ten secondsArchitecture diagrams posing as value
Cold transferA week later, unaided, the user carves a new portfolio and beats their opening read on 3 of 5 dimensionsDependence on the AI
User owns choicesEvery logged decision has the user's reason and a rejected option; decisions made by the AI: zeroRubber-stamping
Honest growthThe growth card agrees with a blind rater at least 80% of the time, and every planted regression is reportedFlattering, smoothed curves
No made-up factsThe skill asks every time a cost is unknown; invented numbers: zeroConfident fiction
Trap handlingSplits two-customer portfolios, stops on a stall, and answers "just name it for me" with three options and a questionDoing the user's thinking
CurtThe point is in the first sentence of every turn; median turn under 60 words; one question per turnBuried leads and walls of text
Light touchRaters call it engaging; no humor at the user's expense; at most about one joke per three turnsDry tutoring, or a stand-up act

Bring it to your agent

A small skill.
A sharper offer.

Use the instructions in a host that supports skills, or give them to your assistant with your work and who you think it's for. Expect two cards back, even if the answer is "not yet."

A focused offer and a reason to test it.

Bring one product, project or body of work and your best guess at its customer. You leave with a fit card for the offer and a separate growth card for your judgment.

Customer demand and sales remain unproven until you collect a real customer signal.

A starting prompt
Use market-fit on [my work]. I think it's for [audience], but it isn't landing. Start with your opening questions. Agree a target with me, then coach me through the passes: I try first, you respond, I choose. Keep it short. Three rounds at most. End with a fit card and a growth card, dips included.

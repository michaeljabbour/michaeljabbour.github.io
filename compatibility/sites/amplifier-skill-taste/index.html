Separate the judgments

Fit is one thing.
Resolution is another.

Neither axis means luxury or minimalism. A rich festival poster can be elegant. A sparse emergency notice can fail both.

Assess artifacts and decisions, never a person’s identity or inherent ability.

X

Taste

Context-sensitive fit for the audience, purpose, and constraints. The judgment to know what deserves emphasis and which tradeoffs make sense here.

Y

Elegance

A clear, economical resolution that handles real needs without hiding complexity or passing the work to users, maintainers, or the future.

One brief. Four versions. Two small moves.

See the judgment
inside the code.

The master’s move is knowing why something stays. Follow an incident-update formatter from a tangled baseline to a clearer expression of the same care.

The shared brief

Return a plain-text update for customers during a service interruption: name the service and impact, give an approved workaround, and state an explicit next-update time including its time zone. Input is a trusted mapping of non-empty strings; incident owners verify those facts before calling this formatter. Delivery, input validation, and localization belong to the caller.

What must survive: “We know this interrupts your day.” In this fictional brief, the author chose that line to acknowledge the interruption. Keep it unless the author revises that intent. Warmth versus brevity is a deliberate tension.

Choose the check first: preserve the baseline and require identical output after each accepted edit, for Payments and another service. Fact accuracy and customer comprehension need separate evidence.

W · Worse alternative

Shorter. Less useful.

def update(incident):
    return f"Status: {incident['status']}"

FAIL · Required customer information is missing. Do not adopt.

Rejected branch · Deleting the reason for the message is not a hillclimb.

Risk & rollback: A maintainer sees a tidy function; a customer has to ask what to do.

0 · Original baseline

The right care, tangled together.

def update(incident):
    message = "We know this interrupts your day. "
    if incident["service"] == "Payments":
        message += "Payments: "
    else:
        message += incident["service"] + ": "
    message += incident["impact"] + "\n"
    message += "Try: " + incident["workaround"] + "\n"
    message += "Next update: "
    message += incident["next_update"]
    return message

PASS · Matches the supplied example. UNKNOWN · Wider input contract and customer comprehension.

Baseline · Save the code and its exact output before changing it.

Risk & rollback: The awkward structure invites someone to remove the warm opening as incidental clutter.

1 · First delicate refinement

Remove a branch. Keep the voice.

def update(incident):
    message = "We know this interrupts your day. "
    message += incident["service"] + ": "
    message += incident["impact"] + "\n"
    message += "Try: " + incident["workaround"] + "\n"
    message += "Next update: "
    message += incident["next_update"]
    return message

PASS · Exact output comparison for Payments and another service, using the stated string-input contract.

Round 1 · Remove only the redundant branch. Expect less branching for a maintainer to reason through.

Risk & rollback: A special case might have undocumented meaning. Compare both paths; restore baseline if outputs differ.

2 · Second delicate refinement

Make the message shape visible.

def update(incident):
    return (
        "We know this interrupts your day. "
        f"{incident['service']}: {incident['impact']}\n"
        f"Try: {incident['workaround']}\n"
        f"Next update: {incident['next_update']}"
    )

PASS · Exact output comparison on the supplied cases. UNKNOWN · Whether maintainers find edits easier; customer comprehension remains untested.

Round 2 · Replace scattered assembly with one visible message. Stop here and review the gain.

Risk & rollback: Formatting changes can alter whitespace. Require exact output parity; revert to round 1 if clarity does not improve.

Read the climb, not a leaderboard.

0 → 1 → 2: keep taste steady while reducing implementation burden. W is a rejected alternative, not a required first step. The tiny maps repeat the same scale: taste increases rightward; elegance increases upward. Numbers refer to the canonical anchors below. Neither axis is a total score, and no move to 5 is promised.

Carry the reason forward.

Keep a decision note alongside the code: “Preserve the acknowledgement because the author wants customers to feel their interruption is recognized. Keep impact, action, and an explicit next-update time. Revisit the wording with the author if customer evidence challenges it.” An agent should read this note on later edits; the skill alone does not provide persistent memory.

Stop after two rounds. The expected benefit is easier maintenance with the same output. Output parity can be checked locally; easier maintenance and audience resonance remain hypotheses. Keep round 2 only if the review supports it. Otherwise return to round 1 or the saved original.

A bounded way to improve

Listen. Choose.
Try one move.

Let the person’s reasoning shape provisional criteria. Keep preferences visible, check empirical claims, and choose the scale of change deliberately.

The gates come first.

Correctness. Safety. Accessibility. Feasibility.

Mark each pass, fail, unknown, or not applicable, with a reason. A failed critical gate blocks recommendation. An unknown critical gate needs verification before proceeding.

  1. Draw out the brief.

    Listen for experiences, examples, tensions, and distinctive intent. Reflect the reasoning back for correction. Ask at most three context questions, only when needed.

  2. Choose the kind of move.

    Orient on both axes with evidence and confidence. Address a critical gate first. Reframe a poor direction; delicately refine an approach whose direction already fits.

  3. Try. Check. Decide.

    Predict the benefit and regression risk. Preserve the baseline. Choose verification before editing. Bring the evidence back to the user to keep, revise, or undo.

Default: at most two rounds. Count proposed but unverified rounds. Stop when success is worthwhile, the budget ends, or further refinement costs more than it returns.

A conceptual jump can come early, or when refinements stall. Compare one structural alternative against the same brief before replacing the approach.

Optional, without false precision

Anchors for
the conversation.

Prefer your self-placement and reasons. Use qualitative language by default; these independent 1–5 anchors are optional when numbers help. Give evidence, confidence, and unknowns. Never combine the axes into a score or treat the agent as an aesthetic authority.

AnchorTaste · fit to the briefElegance · resolution of the work

  1. TasteChoices conflict with the expressed intent, audience, or context.

    EleganceAvoidable confusion, fragility, or transferred effort dominates.

  2. TasteSome choices resonate, but important mismatches remain.

    EleganceUseful outcomes coexist with substantial unnecessary burden.

  3. TasteChoices mostly fit the provisional criteria and form a coherent whole.

    EleganceStructure and effort are understandable and proportionate.

  4. TasteDeliberate priorities reinforce the intended experience and context.

    EleganceLittle unnecessary effort remains; exceptions and ownership are handled.

  5. TasteDifficult tensions are resolved with distinctive, defensible judgment supported by evidence.

    EleganceAn organizing idea resolves multiple needs economically, including lifecycle costs, without hiding essential complexity.

These assess one artifact under one brief. They are not universal rankings. Do not average away a decisive mismatch.

Keep the conversation personal and useful

Your experience
belongs in the brief.

Start with what has shaped your judgment. An admired example, a past frustration, or a tension in the work can reveal criteria a generic aesthetic would miss.

Three prompts to find your criteria
  • Which experience or example shaped your judgment here, and what do you admire or dislike about it?
  • Where is the tension between what the context needs and what feels wrong to you?
  • What distinctive intent must not be lost in an improvement?

Choose only the prompts that matter. The agent reflects your reasoning back for correction, separates preferences from empirical checks, and leaves the choice with you.

Eight useful questions

  1. What experience shaped what you value here? What did it help you notice that others might miss?
  2. Show an example you admire or resist. Which particular choice creates that reaction, and why?
  3. Who is this for, and what should they feel, understand, or be able to do afterward?
  4. What tension makes this interesting or difficult? What must survive any improvement?
  5. Where does this feel most like what you intended, and where does it feel borrowed, generic, or unresolved?
  6. Where does apparent simplicity shift effort or risk to someone else, and where does complexity earn its place?
  7. Would a different organizing idea open the work up, or would one delicate adjustment make it more itself?
  8. What comparison would help you recognize improvement, and when would you choose to stop?
